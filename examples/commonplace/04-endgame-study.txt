# Rook endgame on the kitchen table

I keep a cheap vinyl board next to the kettle and replay Lucena and Philidor
until the coffee cools. Endgame study is the only part of chess that still
feels like carpentry: corresponding squares, opposition, the short side, the
long side, and the moment the defender is in zugzwang.

Lucena wants a bridge. The checking rook swings to the fourth rank, the king
steps out, and the pawn queens because the bridge takes the checks. Philidor
wants a third-rank cut and a patient wait until the pawn advances too far.
I still mix them up when I am tired.

Last week I spent forty minutes on a corresponding-squares problem that
looked like a plumbing diagram. No opening trap, no brilliancy prize. Just
the slow geometry that decides whether a single pawn is a win or a walk
home in the rain.
