The levain wakes before I do. I keep the jar on the second shelf, feed it
equal weights of flour and water, and wait for the levain to dome. If the
levain smells like yogurt and apples, gluten development will be kind later.
If the levain smells like nail polish, I discard most of it and feed again.

I mix the dough, then leave it for a stretch-and-fold every thirty minutes
so the gluten network can tighten without a mixer. The gluten is what lets
the crumb open. A timid gluten window means a tight crumb; an overworked
gluten window tears and the crumb collapses. I aim for a glossy sheet.

The dutch oven does the rest. I preheat the dutch oven until the handle
scares me, then drop the scored loaf onto parchment and lid it. Steam stays
trapped, the crust stays elastic, and the crumb can expand. Twenty minutes
later I pull the lid. The loaf sings. The levain has done its quiet work:
tiny acetic notes, an irregular crumb, and a crust that shatters onto the
board. I still call the leftover paste in the jar levain, not starter, because
that is the word that taught me to wait.
