On the south slope the tea garden wakes before the village.
Rows of low bushes hold their leaves out to the first light.
Pickers walk the terraces with baskets and take only the young tips.
The kiln down the path dries yesterday's harvest so the leaves keep their green.
A kettle on the shed stove is for the workers, not for sale.
We drink that plain garden tea standing up, still tasting of mist and stems.
After noon the slope throws a hard shadow and the picking stops.
The remaining leaves stay on the bush; bitterness comes if you rush them.
In April the first flush is small and expensive.
In August the same garden gives a darker cup and a longer story.
The owner counts baskets, not words, and still calls the hillside a garden
because every bush was planted by a grandparent who drank from the same kettle.
