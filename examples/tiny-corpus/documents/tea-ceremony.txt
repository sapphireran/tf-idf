The tea ceremony begins when the kettle sings, not when the guests sit.
A cloth wipes the bowl in the same three strokes every winter afternoon.
Leaves wait in a paper packet; they are powdered, not the garden's loose tips.
Hot water from the kettle turns the powder into a thick tea that holds a peak.
The host turns the bowl so the guest drinks from the quieter side.
Nobody names the village. Nobody counts baskets. The room is the whole hillside.
Steam is the only weather. Silence is the only clock.
A second pouring is thinner and cooler; the first bowl was the lesson.
When the kettle is empty the cloth is folded and the packet is sealed.
Guests leave tasting foam and charcoal, not mist and stems.
The ceremony does not sell tea. It spends it carefully, one bowl at a time.
