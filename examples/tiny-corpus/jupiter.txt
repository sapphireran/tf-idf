Opposition night is the one evening I drag the telescope onto the fire
escape and pretend the city is a dark-sky site. Jupiter sits high, a bright
coin that does not twinkle. Through the telescope the belts resolve first:
two tan belts across a pale disk, then a hint of the Great Red Spot if the
seeing holds. I refocus the telescope until the belts stop swimming.

Ganymede is the prize. Ganymede is not a spark next to Jupiter so much as a
steady bead I can hold in the same field as the belts. I sketch Ganymede
east of the disk, then ten minutes later Ganymede has slipped, which is the
whole joke of opposition: the moons advertise that the telescope is pointed
at a moving system, not a sticker on the sky.

Neighbors ask what I am photographing. I am not photographing. I am waiting
for the belts to snap sharp and for Ganymede to clear a diffraction spike.
When the seeing collapses I cap the telescope, write "opposition, belts
soft, Ganymede still obvious" in the log, and leave Jupiter to the haze.
The log has more failed opposition nights than pretty ones. I keep the
telescope anyway. Ganymede does not care about my fire escape.
